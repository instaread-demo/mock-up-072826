Range Rover's Electrostatic Sound System Sets New Standard for Automotive Audio Excellence

Listen to audio version of this article
Sponsored by
0:00 / 6:08
Instaread

The $12,570 audio upgrade uses 21 thin-film transducers that respond 1,000 times faster than conventional speakers for unmatched clarity.

Dan Carney,Senior Editor,Design News

July 27, 2026

5 Min Read
The 2027 Range Rover SV Ultra.Jaguar Land Rover

At a Glance

  • Electrostatic transducers respond 1,000 times faster than traditional speakers for superior clarity.
  • The system reduces mass by 90% & adds 20 miles of EV range compared to conventional speakers.
  • Haptic seats and floors complement 21 electrostatic transducers to recreate full audio spectrum.

Watching commercials for new televisions on existing televisions presents the conundrum of how to show a flattering picture for the new product on the presumably inferior older product.

Even harder is describing high-fidelity audio technology via the written word. “Sounds good” does no service to the audio system’s engineers nor to the reader. However, when Range Rover introduced the SV Electrostatic Sound audio system, using electrostatic speaker technology from Warwick Acoustics, the company introduced a step change in automotive audio quality to the market that has to be experienced to be believed.

Range Rover provided that opportunity to Design News at the company’s Gaydon, UK, headquarters, providing a demonstration in a Range Rover SV, the model for which the Electrostatic Sound audio system is a $12,570 option.

For people who can afford the $222,000 Range Rover SV, a $12,000-something stereo option is a reasonable consideration. For the rest of us, the SV Electrostatic Sound system is a glimpse of what is to come: technology that is so superior to existing speakers, with their magnet drivers and speaker cones, that its eventual proliferation seems inevitable.

Related:Range Rover GT Debuts as Electric Fastback With Off-Road Capability and Minimalist Interior

Hearing Pink Floyd’s “Dark Side of the Moon” demonstrated in the Range Rover reminded me of hearing the same song played for the first time on a Sony CDP-101 CD player in my dormitory, absent the background crackle of a record or hiss of a cassette tape. The retail price of that CD player was about $1,000, or about $3,250 today. That is still less than the Electrostatic Sound system’s 12 grand, but the point is that by the end of their production, CD players cost less than $100.

A similar trajectory could put electrostatic speakers like the ones in the Range Rover into mainstream vehicles eventually, and we would be happy for that development. Driven to the event from the airport by a hired driver in a Range Rover, he mentioned that he’d had a Range Rover SV with the Electrostatic system the week before while driving VIPs at Wimbledon.

He said that he’d expected it to be a gimmick and thought he wouldn’t care for the haptic seat shakers. But he found himself missing the system in the vehicle I rode in.

It is worse for Range Rover senior electrical engineer Tilesh Karania. He was pleased that the system had impressed his teenage daughter, which is a hard-earned win for a parent. However, now he doesn’t enjoy the stereo in his own car. “Now, I hate my own car!” he complained.

The electrostatic transducer's installation in the Range Rover. JAGUAR LAND ROVER

What could cause such thoughts? Sitting inside the demonstration vehicle, it was instantly evident that the electrostatic speakers recreate the sounds recorded with near-perfect fidelity, so the listener hears details that would normally go unheard, and each instrument is clearly and distinctly audible.

Related:Land Rover Defender Octa Brings High-Speed Off-Road Tech With 626 HP V8 and Adaptive Suspension

The 21 tiny electrostatic thin film transducers do not displace large volumes of air the way woofers and sub-woofers do when creating bass, so the Electrostatic Sound system uses five bass speakers and haptics in the seats and floors in the Body and Soul Seat and Sensory Floor to create the vibrations that listeners would expect from low-frequency sounds.

“As vehicles evolve into more immersive, software-defined environments, the in-cabin experience is becoming a key point of differentiation,” observed Jorge Reynaga, manager of applications engineering at haptic device supplier Cirrus Logic. “Premium is no longer defined by sound alone; automakers are increasingly seeking to create synchronized, multisensory experiences that combine audio, touch, and intelligent interface feedback to make the interior more intuitive, engaging, and personal.”

Each of the electrostatic speakers contains an ultra‑light, ultra‑responsive 1mm membrane mounted between two perforated metallic plates. The audio signal is applied to those plates, with the membrane responding 1,000 times faster than a traditional speaker. This is why the sound experience is an instantly obvious upgrade to listeners.

Related:Range Rover Sport's Ingenium Engine Pairs Electric Supercharger With Turbo for Instant Power

The transducers are mounted throughout the cabin. Some of them are located in the usual speakers' positions, while others are in seat backs or the headliner. These transducers reduce mass by 90% compared to speakers and similarly slash power consumption. This is worth an added 20 miles of driving range per charge in an EV with a 100 kilowatt-hour battery pack, according to supplier Warwick Acoustics. Plus, the absence of magnetic speaker drivers means that the transducers contain no rare-earth materials.

The sound is the closest thing to perfection that listeners will ever hear. Karania explained that Range Rover’s audio engineers have tuned the system to be neutral, but unfortunately, they’ve included adjustments for bass, mid-range, and treble that will allow owners to spoil the sound if they want. They realize that drivers may have different levels of appreciation for the haptic sound in the seats and floor, so those have five levels of adjustment and can also be turned off completely.

“In the same way audio profiles are tailored today, tactile experiences can be tuned, differentiated, and personalized over time,” Reynaga noted. “That flexibility gives automakers new opportunities across seats, displays, steering controls, and other smart surfaces to shape distinctive in-cabin experiences and express brand identity.”

“Range Rover is proud to be the first automotive brand to offer this unique and exclusive experience to its clients,” said Range Rover Global Managing Director Martin Limpert. “The listening experience is a step change from anything that has been fitted to any vehicle previously, delivering a powerful, emotional and truly immersive experience.”

The rest of us can hope for opportunities to hear electrostatic transducers for ourselves, to tide us over until the technology comes down in price and becomes commonplace.

Dan Carney

Senior Editor, Design News

Dan’s coverage of the auto industry over three decades has taken him to the racetracks, automotive engineering centers, vehicle simulators, wind tunnels, and crash-test labs of the world.

Dan is a winner of the International Motor Press Association's Ken Purdy Award for automotive writing, as well as the National Motorsports Press Association's award for magazine writing and the Washington Automotive Press Association's Golden Quill award.

He has been published in The New York Times, NBC News, Motor Trend, Popular Mechanics, The Washington Post, Hagerty, AutoTrader.com, Maxim, RaceCar Engineering, AutoWeek, Virginia Living, and others.

Dan has authored books on the Honda S2000 and Dodge Viper sports cars and contributed automotive content to the consumer finance book, Fight For Your Money.
