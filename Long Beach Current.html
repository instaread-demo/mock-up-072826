CSULB Law Society launches first undergraduate law review in CSU system

After years of failed attempts, students bring an undergraduate law review to campus.

The campus Law Society made CSULB history this year by launching the first undergraduate law review in the CSU system and the fourth program among public universities in California.

A group of 14 students published the first issue of the Beach Undergraduate Law Review on May 1, titled “Federalism and Fundamental Rights: Reexamining Constitutional and Civil Liberties in Modern America.” 

An undergraduate law review is a scholarly journal led, edited and written by students. Such publications are not typically found at the undergraduate level because they are difficult to maintain and require a highly skilled, detail-oriented team to operate, said Nicholas Nieto, Law Society president and editor-in-chief of the student-run law review.

“This is something that is special to us, something that makes us different from other schools,” he said.

The first issue contains four entries, covering topics ranging from federal immigration enforcement to a comparative analysis between federalism and nationalism. 

Nieto said the idea emerged during a conversation with Law Society faculty adviser Jason Whitehead, who noted that past student efforts to start a law review had fallen through. Nieto said he viewed that as a challenge.

“I started researching how to get this off the ground and reached out to other schools,” Nieto said. “I got into contact with the editor-in-chief over at UCLA Law Review, not their undergraduate law, but their actual published official law review, which was an absolute blessing.”

Nieto added that he also connected with USC’s undergraduate law review and expressed gratitude to both institutions for their time and support.

Nikki Majidi, the law review’s co-founder and senior editor, had worked closely with Nieto on the legislative policy agenda during her time as Associated Students, Inc. president. So when he approached her about starting an undergraduate law review together, she quickly said yes.

“We worked tirelessly to put the applications out. We sat in a coffee shop for hours deciding who was going to be on our team and what was going to be the future of law review,” Majidi said.

Thu Chung, a third-year political science major and one of two lead editors on the team, said she has enjoyed being part of the publication. 

As lead editor, Chung oversaw the two notes her team was working on. She said note editors focus on the flow of the piece, while text editors handle Bluebooking and grammar. She added that she intends to remain on the team next year and was pleased with the experience.

Whitehead emphasized the historic significance of establishing an undergraduate law review on campus, noting previous attempts in 2008, 2011 and 2013. 

“I’ve been the faculty adviser since 2006. This has been the most momentous year in the history of Law Society on this campus since I’ve been part of it,” Whitehead said.
